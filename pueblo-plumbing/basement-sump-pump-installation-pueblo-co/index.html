    Frequently Asked Questions About Basement Sump Pump Installation in Pueblo

    Replacing a pump in an existing pit usually runs $500 to $1,200 including the pump and check valve. A brand new pit through the slab falls between $1,500 and $3,500, and a battery backup adds roughly $600 to $1,500. Most people add the backup.

    For some homeowners, a same-model pump swap is a fair weekend job. Cutting a new pit or adding a backup is not, because those mistakes end in flooding, cracked slabs or electrical hazards, and fixing them costs more than paying for the install up front. Those belong to a pro.

    Changing out a pump in an existing pit takes about two to three hours. Plan on most of a day for a new pit with a slab cut and patch, since the concrete needs a little time to firm up before the pump goes into service.

    A primary pump does the everyday work. The secondary, usually battery powered, sits beside it as a backup, and it kicks on if the primary fails, loses power, or gets overwhelmed by more water than it can move during a heavy storm. Each pump gets its own check valve.

    Discharge belongs in the yard, a dry well, or an approved drainage area at least 10 feet from the foundation. Tying a sump pump into the sanitary sewer is not allowed in most places, and it can draw a fine.

    Our installs carry a written warranty on both parts and labor. The exact term is spelled out in your service agreement, and the pump manufacturer adds its own coverage, which on a quality cast iron pump usually runs three to five years from the date of purchase. We register it for you.

    Plan on 4 to 8 hours for a standard install. A new pit with slab cutting can stretch it to a full day, and tricky electrical access or a long discharge run through a finished wall adds time on top of that. You get a time estimate first.

    A straight pump replacement generally goes without a permit. New pits, new discharge routes and new circuits may, so we look up the local rules and file for a permit when one is needed.
