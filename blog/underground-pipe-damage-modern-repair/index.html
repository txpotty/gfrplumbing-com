    Underground Pipe Damage: Common Causes and Modern Repair Solutions

    By Luis Ortega · August 31, 2026 · 6 min read

    The pipes you never see do some of the hardest work in the house. The water service line runs from the meter to the house, usually 3/4 or 1 inch pipe buried below the frost line. A sewer lateral carries everything from the drains out to the city main or the septic tank. Both sit several feet underground, and either one can fail with very little warning. Fixing one rarely means trenching the yard anymore.

    What Damages Buried Pipes in Colorado

    Tree roots cause more sewer trouble than anything else. Willows and silver maples are the worst offenders, though plenty of ornamental trees go after sewer lines too. A hairline crack or loose joint leaks warm, nutrient-rich vapor, and the roots follow it right in. Inside, they grow into a mat that snags waste and can eventually split the pipe.

    Expansive clay soils are a well-known problem in parts of Colorado, swelling with moisture and lifting slabs, basement floors, and buried pipes.

    Soil movement does damage slowly. Clay that swells after rain and shrinks in a drought keeps working against buried rock and hard layers that do not move at all. Over years that cycle shifts joints and cracks rigid pipe, leaving sags, which plumbers call bellies, where waste sits and builds up. Heavy trucks parked over a shallow line and poor bedding during construction add to the problem.

    Pipe material matters as well. Older neighborhoods may still have clay tile or cast iron sewer lines. Some houses also have galvanized steel water service lines that rust from the inside out. ABS and PVC, standard from the 1970s onward, will not rust, though they still crack and separate at the joints.

    Homes from the late 1970s through the mid 1990s may still have polybutylene supply lines, which are known to fail at fittings.

    Warning Signs of Underground Pipe Damage

    Buried pipe problems show up indirectly, through other fixtures. Watch for these:

    • Repeat backups in more than one fixture, starting with whatever drain sits lowest.
    • A gurgling toilet when the washing machine drains.
    • Sewer smell in the yard or around a cleanout.
    • A green or soggy strip of lawn that stands out in a dry spell.
    • Sinkholes or depressions along the path of the line.
    • A water meter that keeps moving with every fixture off, pointing to a service line leak.
    • Low water pressure through the whole house.

    Modern Repair Methods

    Diagnosis comes first. For a sewer line, we push a waterproof camera through the cleanout and record the full run. The footage shows roots, cracks, offsets and bellies, and a locator at the camera head marks the depth and location of each one from the surface. For water service lines, pressure testing and acoustic leak detection narrow down where the water is getting out.

    Cured-in-place lining slides a resin-soaked felt liner into the old sewer, inflates it and lets it cure into a new jointless pipe inside the old one. The finished liner usually loses only a quarter inch or so of diameter. It seals cracks and root entry points and usually needs only the cleanout for access. Pipe bursting pulls new HDPE through the line while a cone-shaped head breaks the old pipe outward. Bursting suits pipe too broken to line. Spot repair means digging up one section to replace a single broken joint or belly. For water service, directional boring runs a new line from meter to house with a small pit at each end.

    Cost comes down to footage and depth. Expect $150 to $400 for the camera inspection itself. Spot sewer repairs often land between $1,500 and $4,500, trenchless lining or bursting of a full residential lateral commonly runs about $4,000 to $12,000, and replacing the water service usually comes in around $1,500 to $5,000. Per foot, trenchless can cost as much as digging or more, but it saves the driveway and landscaping from being torn out and rebuilt.

    When to Call a Professional

    Drains that keep backing up, or a meter showing a leak you cannot find, call for a professional inspection. Most underground jobs need a permit and an 811 utility locate, plus careful digging around gas and sprinkler lines.
