    Why a Sewer Camera Inspection Belongs on Every Colorado Home Inspection

    By Andrew Blake · August 13, 2026 · 5 min read

    A general home inspection covers a lot. The inspector checks the roof, the electrical panel, the HVAC and the water heater, along with whatever plumbing is exposed. The inside of the main sewer usually gets skipped. Running water in every fixture for 20 minutes proves very little, because a line can drain normally for a short test and still be cracked, sagging or full of roots. A sewer scope is one of the best add-ons a buyer can order before closing.

    What a Sewer Camera Inspection Involves

    The plumber feeds a waterproof camera on a flexible push cable through a cleanout, usually near the foundation or out in the front yard. The video records as the camera moves down the pipe, and you can watch it live on the monitor. Most units have a locating transmitter in the head, so we can paint the exact spot and depth of a problem on the grass. Plan on 30 to 60 minutes. Ask for the video file and a written summary before the plumber leaves.

    No cleanout? We can sometimes go in from a roof vent or through a toilet flange. That access may add to the price. Most residential scopes run $150 to $350 when there is a cleanout in reach.

    Before the visit, ask the listing agent for any records of past sewer work. Invoices for root cutting or paperwork from a previous lining job tell us a lot. With that history in hand, we know where to slow the camera down and can check whether the old repairs are still holding. Sellers often have it in a drawer.

    Ask the seller, too, whether the house has ever had a main line backup into the basement or the lowest shower. A yes there tells us to look hard at the first 20 feet out from the foundation. Honest answers save time.

    What the Camera Can Reveal

    Sewer problems are expensive, and you rarely see them from inside the house. A camera can turn up:

    • Root intrusion at the joints, most often near big, older trees.
    • Bellies or sags where the pipe settled and waste collects.
    • Offset or separated joints from years of soil movement.
    • Cracked or collapsed sections in clay tile or cast iron.
    • Heavy corrosion and scale inside older cast iron.
    • Grease buildup that points to ongoing maintenance.
    • Bad past repairs, like two different pipe materials joined without the right coupling.

    Why It Matters for Colorado Buyers

    Housing in Colorado ranges from older homes with galvanized and cast iron pipe to fast-growing suburbs built with copper and PEX.

    Every era of construction brings its own sewer risks. In older parts of town, the original clay or cast iron is often still in service decades past its expected life. Cast iron under the slab was standard in many 1960s to 1980s houses, and it corrodes from the inside until the bottom of the pipe splits open. Newer ABS and PVC is not immune either. Bad bedding and clay that moves can still sag the line or pull a joint apart.

    Trees add another wrinkle. Shade trees help curb appeal, but their roots go hunting for moisture in the sewer. Rural lots and some edge-of-town properties use septic instead. There the camera shows the condition of the line out to the tank, and that footage goes nicely with a separate septic inspection.

    Septic systems and private wells are common in the mountains and rural plains of Colorado, outside the reach of municipal sewer.

    How the Results Help You Negotiate

    Sewer repairs cost real money. Figure $1,500 to $4,500 for a spot repair, and $4,000 to $12,000 or more for a new lateral or a trenchless liner. Finding a problem before closing gives you options. The seller can make the repair or give you a credit, or you can decide the house is not the one. If the line looks good, you get a baseline video to compare against later. A seller who scopes before listing gets to handle any repair on their own timeline, not in the middle of a tense negotiation.

    When to Call a Professional

    Book the scope early in your inspection contingency period. You want room on the calendar for repair quotes in case the footage turns up a problem. A licensed plumber, not a general inspector, is the right person for this, because we can read the footage, pinpoint each defect and price the real fix.
