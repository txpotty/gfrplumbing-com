    Pressure-Assisted Toilet Installation in Parker

    Pressure-assisted toilets have a sealed vessel inside the tank that uses line pressure to push the flush. Below about 20 psi, they flush poorly. Before suggesting one, we put a gauge on a hose bib, and we mention the noise, because the flush carries through the whole house. They rarely clog.

    Standard Toilet Installation in Parker

    A standard floor-mount toilet is a two-hour job when the flange is sound. We shut off the stop, drain the tank and pull the old bowl. After scraping the old wax off, we check the flange for cracks, set new stainless bolts, and lower the new toilet straight down onto a fresh seal so it compresses evenly. The old one leaves with us.

    ADA-Compliant Toilet Installation in Parker

    ADA toilets put the seat 17 to 19 inches off the floor, and the centerline sits 16 to 18 inches from the side wall. That spacing leaves room for a grab bar. We measure the existing drain before ordering, since moving a drain two inches to hit the ADA centerline means opening the floor. Elongated bowls are standard here.

    Two-Piece Toilet Installation in Parker

    Two-piece toilets come with the tank and bowl separate, joined by a rubber gasket and two or three bolts. We assemble them on the floor before setting. Even tightening, alternating sides, matters more than force, since uneven pressure either cracks the china or leaves one bolt weeping. It should not wobble.

    Toilet Brands We Install in Parker

    We install Kohler, TOTO, American Standard, Gerber and most other brands sold at supply houses and home centers. Each one has its own parts quirks.     New Construction Toilet Installation in Parker

    On new builds we set toilets after the finish floor is down, never before. A flange belongs flush with or just above finished flooring.     Frequently Asked Questions About Toilet Installation in Parker

    Most homeowners in Parker, CO pay $200 to $500 to have a toilet installed, fixture not included. Removing a stubborn old unit adds time. Rusted bolts get cut off, and a cast iron flange that cracks on the way out needs a repair ring, both adding labor.

    Watch the base and the tank. Water that reappears on the floor after you wipe it up points to a cracked bowl or a failed seal. A seal can be replaced for far less than a new toilet, so we check that first, but a crack in the china means replacement, since there is no lasting repair for cracked porcelain.

    Cracks in the tank or bowl are the clearest sign. Some cracks are hard to see at first. Look for a slow drip on the floor that comes back after you dry it, or frequent clogs on an early low-flow model with a narrow trapway. So is a toilet you have rebuilt twice in three years.

    Lower water bills come first. An old 3.5 gallon toilet uses almost three times the water of a 1.28 gallon model. Newer bowls also have wider glazed trapways that clog less, and the smoother porcelain stays cleaner between scrubbings than an old pitted bowl ever did.

    Homeowners set basic toilets all the time. The trouble spots are flange height and bolt torque. A low flange needs an extender ring, and one extra turn on a nut can split the base, which is why new floors and wall-hung units should go to a pro.

    A one-piece toilet is cast as a single unit, so there is no tank-to-bowl joint to leak. Expect a higher price and more weight. A two-piece bolts the tank to the bowl with a rubber gasket, which makes it lighter to carry up stairs and cheaper to fix, since you can replace just the tank or just the bowl if one cracks. Both flush well.

    Figure one to two hours for a standard floor-mount toilet. Bigger jobs take a day or more. Wall-hung systems and drain relocations on a slab mean opening walls or floors, so those get scheduled as their own project with time for patches to cure.

    Mountain homes in Colorado frequently rely on private wells, which may need treatment for iron, manganese, or natural minerals. Hard water leaves scale in the rim jets and on the fill valve over time. Flush power drops as jets clog. A toilet with a well-glazed trapway and an easy-to-reach fill valve holds up better, and a cup of vinegar left in the overflow tube overnight now and then keeps the rim jets open.
