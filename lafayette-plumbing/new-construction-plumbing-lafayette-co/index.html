    Frequently Asked Questions About New Construction Plumbing in Lafayette

    Most new single-family homes in the Lafayette area cost between $12,000 and $25,000 to plumb, with larger custom homes running higher. Each added bathroom adds several thousand dollars. Tankless heaters and gas runs add to it. Our bid is fixed per phase, so the number you sign is the number you pay unless the plans change.

    Workmanship is covered under our written warranty, and the length is spelled out in your contract. Fixtures carry their own manufacturer warranties. PEX and water heater warranties can run ten years or longer if the matching fittings are used, so we keep each system one brand.

    PEX handles most water supply in new homes. It flexes and needs fewer fittings in the walls than copper. Drains and vents are usually Schedule 40 PVC or ABS, depending on what the local code allows and what the plans specify for the building. Copper still shows up at water heaters.

    PEX-A is our default for water supply in Colorado homes. It handles heat well and resists the scale and pinhole problems copper can get with aggressive water. We run drains in Schedule 40 PVC. Joints under the slab are kept few, and every turn uses a long sweep so a cable can pass.

    Most jurisdictions inspect plumbing three times. First comes underground, with drains holding a water or air test before the pour. The rough or top-out inspection happens before insulation and drywall, and the final comes once fixtures are set, the water heater is running and the gas is tested. Some add a gas test.

    Yes, we take single-phase jobs. We can handle only the underground, only top-out, or only trim. If another plumber started the job, we inspect and test what is already in place before we tie into it, because we will not put our name on pipe we have not seen hold pressure.

    Scheduling causes most of the trouble. Pushed pours and framing changes tend to pile phases together. Late plan changes are next, since moving a tub after framing means more drilling and another inspection. Every change gets priced in writing before we move pipe.

    Some jurisdictions let owner-builders plumb a home they will live in. Permits and inspections still apply. Inspectors apply the same code, and a failed underground can cost more in delay than hiring it out. Gas piping often requires a license.
